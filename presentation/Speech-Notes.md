# BiteBuddy: Speech Notes

About 7 minutes in total, roughly 40 seconds per slide.
Suggested split: Speaker 1 slides 1–4, Speaker 2 slides 5–7, Speaker 3 slides 8–11.

---

## Slide 1: Title (20 s)

Hello everyone. Our project is about how interface design affects attention and decisions.
We are Shahira, Bashair and Mehak.
We built two versions of the same mobile app and compared them.

## Slide 2: Problem Statement (40 s)

We use our phones to make quick decisions every day.
When a screen is cluttered, things are hard to find.
People need more effort, they are slower, and they make more mistakes.
So we asked four questions, shown on the right.
Does a busy design pull attention away?
Does it change how accurately and how fast people decide?
Which design finishes an order faster?
And which one feels harder, and which do people prefer?
Our idea was simple: build the same app twice, one clean and one cluttered, and measure the difference.

## Slide 3: Literature Review (45 s)

We read five recent ACM papers, from 2024 to 2026.
Together, they tell us four things.
One: layout and emphasis guide where people look.
Two: when the mind is busy, people notice less.
Three: how options are grouped changes how people go through them.
Four: we can measure this through behaviour, like clicks and time. We do not need an eye tracker.
But none of these papers tested a food-ordering app on a phone, clean versus cluttered.
So we built one.

## Slide 4: Our Solution (40 s)

Our solution has four steps.
First, we built one food-ordering app, called BiteBuddy, two times.
Second, we changed only the design. One version is clean, one is cluttered.
Third, 12 students use both versions. They find a meal, choose a meal, and place an order.
Fourth, we measure time, accuracy, taps, workload and which design they prefer.
The items, prices and steps are exactly the same.
So any difference comes from the design only.

## Slide 5: Design B, Low Complexity (35 s)

This is Design B, the clean version.
On Home, the categories are clear.
On Menu, items are grouped into sections.
On the item screen, there is one main focus: the name and price.
And the cart summary is neat and aligned.
This design follows good HCI rules.

## Slide 6: Design A, High Complexity (35 s)

This is Design A, the cluttered version.
It has the same items and the same buttons.
But Home has no categories and some fake buttons that do nothing.
The menu is in a mixed order, with five different card styles.
On the item screen, the price is far from the name.
And in the cart, the total is smaller than the subtotal.
We broke these rules on purpose.

## Slide 7: AHCI Principles, B vs A (40 s)

This table compares the two designs, rule by rule.
For example, visual hierarchy: in B, the name and price stand out. In A, everything fights for attention.
For organisation: B groups items by category. A shows one mixed list.
For similarity: in B, buttons look clickable. In A, fake buttons look real.
So B follows each principle, and A breaks it.

## Slide 8: Survey Results (40 s)

These are our results. *(Say if still true: "These are sample numbers for now.")*
The chart shows the time for each task.
Design B, in blue, was faster in all three tasks.
On the right, you can see more results.
Design A had more wrong taps, fewer correct picks and a higher workload score.
And 10 out of 12 students preferred Design B.

## Slide 9: What the Results Tell Us (45 s)

Now let's answer our four questions.
RQ1: The cluttered design pulled attention away. People tapped the wrong things more.
RQ2: Clutter hurt decisions. People picked the correct item less often, and they were slower.
RQ3: The clean design finished the full order about 35 percent faster.
RQ4: The cluttered design felt almost twice as hard, and most people preferred the clean one.
We also have some limitations. Our sample is small. Design A changes many things together. And we measure taps and time, not eye movement.

## Slide 10: Conclusion (35 s)

Our main message: design is not just decoration.
Good hierarchy, grouping and consistency help people focus, decide faster and feel less effort.
So we have four recommendations for designers.
Group content into clear categories.
Keep one main focus on each card.
Use one consistent style for buttons and controls.
And use text that is easy to read.

Thank you for listening. We are happy to take your questions.
